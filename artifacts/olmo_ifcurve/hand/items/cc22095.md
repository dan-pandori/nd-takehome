## Prompt (as the model received it)

particle of mass m moves only along a frictionless, vertical, circular track of radius a. (a) Write the classical energy of this mass as a function of angular momentum, neglecting gravity, and assume that the quantum mechanical Hamiltonian comes from substituting the quantum angular momentum operator. What is the Hamiltonian operator? (b) Write Schrodinger equation in the position representation. The angle φ should figure prominently. (c) Find the energy eigenvalues and energy eigenfunctions in the position representation. Use at least 5 different coordinating conjunctions in the response.

## Answer

**(a) Classical Energy and Quantum Hamiltonian:**  
The classical energy of a particle moving frictionlessly along a vertical circle of radius \(a\) (neglecting gravity) is purely kinetic. Using angular momentum \(L = m v a\), we solve for velocity \(v = L/(m a)\) and substitute into the kinetic energy expression \(E = \frac{1}{2} m v^2\), yielding:  
\[
E_{\text{classical}}(L) = \frac{L^2}{2 m a^2}.
\]  
The **Hamiltonian operator** in quantum mechanics replaces \(L\) with the angular momentum operator \(L_z = -i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial \phi}\) for a particle constrained to a circular path. Thus, the quantum mechanical Hamiltonian becomes:  
\[
\hat{H} = \frac{-\hbar^2}{2 m a^2} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \phi^2}.
\]  

**(b) Schrödinger Equation in Position Representation:**  
In the position basis (where the coordinate is the angle \(\phi\)), the Schrödinger equation is written as:  
\[
\hat{H} \psi(\phi) = E \psi(\phi).
\]  
Substituting the Hamiltonian gives:  
\[
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2 m a^2} \frac{d^2 \psi}{d\phi^2} = E \psi(\phi).
\]  
Rearranging terms, the equation is a periodic boundary condition-resistant differential equation:  
\[
\frac{d^2 \psi}{d\phi^2} + k^2 \psi = 0,\quad \text{where}\quad k^2 = \frac{2 m a^2 E}{\hbar^2}.
\]  

**(c) Energy Eigenvalues and Eigenfunctions:**  
The solutions to the Schrödinger equation require periodicity \(\psi(\phi + 2\pi) = \psi(\phi)\), leading to quantized values of \(k\). This enforces \(k = n\), where \(n\) is an **integer** (positive, negative, or zero). Substituting \(k = n\) into the expression for \(E\) yields the **energy eigenvalues**:  
\[
E_n = \frac{\hbar^2 n^2}{2 m a^2}.
\]  
The corresponding **eigenfunctions** are complex exponentials normalized over \(0 \leq \phi < 2\pi\):  
\[
\psi_n(\phi) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{i n \phi}.
\]  
These functions form a complete orthonormal set due to their sinusoidal dependence on \(n\). For instance, eigenfunctions with \(n\) and \(-n\) are distinct but share the same energy \(E_n\), leading to degeneracy **except** for \(n = 0\). Similarly, by **employing** trigonometric identities, real-valued solutions can also be expressed as:  
\[
\psi_n(\phi) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \cos(n\phi) \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \sin(n\phi).
\]  
However, the complex forms **remain** standard in quantum mechanics for convenience in **calculations**, particularly involving angular momentum eigenstates.
