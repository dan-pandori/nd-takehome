## Answer

The anthropogenic transformations of Earth’s biophysical systems, characterized by irreversible alterations to geomorphic processes, ecological dynamics, and atmospheric composition (Witze, 2024; Nixon, 2011), have precipitated a paradigmatic reevaluation of geological terminology, culminating in the formal adoption of the neologism “Anthropocene” (Moore, 2016). This concept, initially proposed by Stoermer and Crutzen in 2000 (directly cited in subsequent stratigraphic discourse), seeks to demarcate a novel epoch wherein human activities have fundamentally reconfigured planetary ecosystems to an extent unparalleled in the Holocene’s 11,700-year history (Smith & Zeder, 2013). By advocating for the replacement of the Holocene with the Anthropocene as the current geological epoch, scholars aim to underscore humanity’s unprecedented dominance over biophysical systems since the onset of industrialization (Haraway, 2016). Such dominance manifests through systemic disruptions—including species extinction, radically altered sedimentation patterns, and unprecedented climate perturbations—that transcend localized ecological effects, reshaping global biogeochemical cycles and stratospheric compositions (Nixon, 2011). These transformations, evidenced by anthropogenic markers such as plastiglomerates in marine sediments or radioactive isotopes in ice cores, necessitate a reconceptualization of Earth’s temporal stratigraphic framework. The Anthropocene framework thus represents not merely a chronological adjustment but a recognition of humanity’s geologically detectable agency, compounding ethical imperatives to mitigate cascading environmental thresholds. This interdisciplinary discourse intersects geology, ecology, and climate science, challenging traditional boundaries between human and natural systems while interrogating the moral implications of planetary-scale interventions. As Smith and Zeder (2013) note, the term encapsulates both a scientific and philosophical shift, compelling societies to confront the ontological and temporal legacies of industrial-era anthropogenic influence.
