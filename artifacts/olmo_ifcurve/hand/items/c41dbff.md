### When Did Humans Realize Space Is a Dark Void?  

The transition from viewing space as a blue expanse to understanding it as a "void of darkness" unfolded gradually, driven by scientific discoveries and technological advancements:  

1. **Ancient to Medieval Eras**:  
   - Pre-scientific cultures often perceived the sky as a celestial dome or part of Earth’s atmosphere. Many associated its blue color with earthly elements (e.g., water or air). The concept of "outer space" as we know it did not yet exist; the universe was often thought to be finite and enclosed.  

2. **17th Century**:  
   - **Galileo’s telescopic observations** (1609–1610) revealed that celestial bodies (the Moon, Jupiter’s moons) were not smooth or divine but had physical features. However, this did not immediately clarify the nature of *empty space*.  
   - The idea of a **vacuum** began to emerge with experiments by Evangelista Torricelli (1643), showing that air pressure could create vacuums—a concept initially met with skepticism.  

3. **19th Century Scientific Breakthroughs**:  
   - **James Clerk Maxwell** (1861–1862) formulated the theory of electromagnetic waves, which could propagate through a vacuum, challenging the need for a medium like the luminiferous aether.  
   - **Lord Rayleigh** (1871) explained the **blue sky** as a result of Rayleigh scattering in Earth’s atmosphere. This implied that *beyond* the atmosphere, there would be no light scattering, leading to darkness.  
   - By the late 1800s, astronomers like **Hermann Minkowski** and **Albert Einstein** (early 20th century) formalized the concept of a **vacuum-filled universe**, removing the need for an aether.  

4. **20th Century Space Exploration**:  
   - **Yuri Gagarin**’s 1961 orbital flight provided the first direct human observation of Earth suspended in a **black void**, a view confirmed by subsequent missions.  
   - **Lunar landings** (1969–1972) and **Hubble Space Telescope** imagery (1990 onward) further cemented the understanding of space as a near-vacuum filled with starlight and distant galaxies, but otherwise dark.  

5. **Public Perception**:  
   - Before the 20th century, many still conflated the blue sky with "outer space," partly due to artistic depictions (e.g., blue heavens in religious art). Scientific literacy and media coverage of space missions gradually shifted public understanding, replacing the "blue sky" myth with the reality of the cosmic dark.  

---

### Key Takeaway:  
The realization that space is dark and starlit required rejecting ancient and medieval cosmologies. It relied on discoveries in **optics**, **Atmospheric science**, and **space exploration**, culminating in the 20th century when empirical evidence from telescopes and astronauts definitively revealed the void beyond Earth’s atmosphere.
