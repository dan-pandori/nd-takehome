## Answer

Confidence is essential for fractional reserve banking to function smoothly. Banks rely on the knowledge that not all depositors will demand their funds simultaneously; this expectation allows them to lend out most deposits, creating new money through the money-multiplier effect. If sudden panic-driven withdrawals occur—a bank run—liquidity shortages force banks to halt lending, jeopardizing the entire system. Maintaining public trust ensures stable deposits and prevents destabilizing rushes for cash. Confidence.  

Productivity underpins the economic rationale for lending. When banks lend money, those funds must generate value through investments, businesses, or consumer spending. Repayments replenish reserves and enable further lending, sustaining the cycle of credit expansion. Without productive uses for loans, defaults rise, reserves shrink, and the banking system’s ability to create money collapses. Economic growth driven by efficient resource allocation ensures loans remain viable, supporting the system’s health. Productivity.  

Reserves are the foundation of fractional reserve banking’s liquidity. Banks must hold enough reserves to meet expected withdrawals while lending the rest. If reserves fall below anticipated needs—due to poor management or external shocks—the system risks insolvency. Reserve adequacy, balanced against lending activity, ensures stability and credibility. Central banks often set minimum reserve ratios to safeguard this balance, but the underlying principle remains: reserves must reliably back deposits. Reserves.  

Solvency ensures banks can withstand financial shocks while continuing operations. Even with proper reserve management, unexpected losses from loan defaults or market crashes can erode capital. A solvent bank maintains sufficient equity to absorb losses without collapsing, protecting depositors and allowing lending to persist. Solvency hinges on sound risk assessment and economic conditions that prevent widespread financial distress. Solvency.
