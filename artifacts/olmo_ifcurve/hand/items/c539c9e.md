### **When Did Humans Learn Space Is a Dark Void?**  

The misconception that "outer space is blue like the sky" likely stems from early Earth-based observations. Here’s how humans gradually uncovered the truth:  

#### **1. Ancient and Medieval Views**  
- **Firmament Beliefs**: Many ancient cultures (e.g., Greeks, Babylonians) imagined a solid "celestial dome" or firmament holding the stars and planets, often colored blue or crystalline. The atmosphere itself was rarely conceptualized separately from Earth’s surface.  
- **Lack of Spaceflight**: Without venturing beyond the atmosphere, humans assumed space shared the sky’s daytime color.  

#### **2. Rayleigh Scattering and Atmospheric Science (Late 1800s)**  
- **John William Strutt Rayleigh** (1870s–1890s) mathematically explained that Earth’s atmosphere scatters sunlight’s shorter blue wavelengths, making the sky appear blue. This implied that **space**, devoid of air, would lack such scattering—and thus appear dark.  
- **Key Insight**: If the "blue sky" relied on air molecules, removing air (as in space) would eliminate the color.  

#### **3. High-Altitude Experiments (Early 20th Century)**  
- **Balloon Ascents**: By the 1930s–1940s, scientists in stratospheric balloons (e.g., **Auguste Piccard**) observed the sky transitioning from blue to black above **18–20 km**. This showed the atmosphere’s role in scattering light.  
- **Sounding Rockets & Satellites (1950s–60s)**: Instruments on rockets confirmed space’s near-perfect darkness beyond the atmosphere.  

#### **4. Human Spaceflight (1960s)**  
- **Astronaut Testimony**: Mercury, Gemini, and Apollo astronauts directly observed the **pitch-black void of space**, punctuated by stars, planets, and galaxies. For example, during the 1969 Apollo 11 mission, Buzz Aldrin described space as "completely black."  
- **Photography**: Early space images (e.g., from NASA’s **OSO-1 satellite**, 1972) showed the darkness of space, contrasting with Earth’s bright atmosphere.  

#### **5. Cultural Shift**  
- **Pre-1900s**: The idea of a "blue celestial ocean" persisted in art and literature (e.g., Romantic paintings of starry nights with deep blue backdrops).  
- **Post-1970s**: Scientific consensus solidified that space is mostly **vacuum**, with light traveling unimpeded—resulting in darkness except where stars/galaxies emit it.  

---

### **Why Did Early Humans Think It Was Blue?**  
They conflated Earth’s atmosphere (the source of the blue sky) with the entirety of space, assuming the universe extended the environment they knew. It took centuries of optical physics, high-altitude experiments, and human space exploration to correct this intuitive error. Today, we know space’s darkness is a result of its near-perfect vacuum and the finite light from distant objects.
