## Answer

Condicyon is the central plea here, an urgent appeal to empathy and responsibility. The speaker invokes the father’s physical and emotional vulnerability—“strycken wyth blyndnesse and destytute”—to highlight his desperate state. The archaic spelling (“condicyon”) and phrasing (“reflecte upon”) anchor the text in Early Modern English, suggesting a religious or literary context where such formality underscores moral gravity. The imperative tone (“Shall I forsake hym?”) challenges the listener’s conscience, framing abandonment as a moral failing. Condicyon.  

Fader lies at the heart of this entreaty, evoking filial duty and familial loyalty. The father’s “latter yeeres” (later years) symbolize a time when care is due, yet he faces “infirmity” and “penurye” (poverty). The rhetorical question—“Shall I wrastle wyth” (wrestle with) his suffering—implies that to neglect him would compound his already dire struggles. The speaker positions themselves as a potential alleviator, contrasting their capacity to act with the father’s helplessness. Fader.  

Comforte is positioned as the antidote to the father’s deprivation, framing mercy as both practical and sacred. The speaker vows to fill his father’s later years with “joye,” contrasting sharply with the bleakness of “deystute” and “blinde[n]nesse.” The dichotomy between “wrestle” (struggle violently) and “rende” (provide/offer) underscores a choice between despair and compassion. While the language is steeped in antiquated terms, its emotional core remains timeless: the obligation to protect the vulnerable. Comforte.
