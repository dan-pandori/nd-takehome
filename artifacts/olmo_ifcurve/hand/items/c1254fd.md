### Regarding humanity’s understanding of outer space as a dark void:  
The idea that outer space is a **dark void illuminated only by stars and galaxies** gradually emerged over centuries of scientific inquiry. Here’s a breakdown of key developments:  

#### **Early Beliefs (Pre-17th Century):**  
- Many ancient civilizations (e.g., Babylonians, Greeks) viewed the sky as a **celestial sphere** composed of incorruptible material, often thought to be crystalline or ethereal.  
- The **color of Earth’s sky** (blue due to atmospheric scattering) was likely conflated with the "space" beyond the atmosphere. Some may have imagined celestial regions as uniformly bright or extending the terrestrial sky’s hue. For example, medieval scholars sometimes described the firmament (the "sky") as a luminous, translucent dome.  

#### **1600s–1609: Galileo’s Observations**  
- **Galileo Galilei**, using his telescope, observed the **Moon’s phases** and **dark regions** (mare), demonstrating that celestial bodies are solid and not part of a luminous, uniform "sky." His findings challenged the notion of a bright or blue celestial realm.  
- He also noted that **planets** (like Jupiter and Saturn) and their moons appeared as **dark disks** against a **black background**, suggesting space itself might be dark.  

#### **17th Century: Newton and the Nature of Light**  
- **Isaac Newton**’s experiments with prisms and light (1666–1670) revealed that **white light splits into colors**, including blue. However, he also showed that **light requires a medium** to travel—a concept later refined by theories of the "luminiferous aether."  
- This era began distinguishing between Earth’s **atmosphere** (responsible for the blue sky) and the **vacuum of space**, though acceptance of a vacuum as "dark" was still debated.  

#### **19th Century: Atmospheric Science and Rayleigh Scattering**  
- In **1867**, **John Tyndall** experimentally confirmed **Rayleigh scattering**, explaining why Earth’s sky is blue (shorter blue wavelengths scatter more in the atmosphere). This clarified that **space’s darkness** arises from the **absence of an atmosphere** to scatter light—space itself is not inherently blue.  
- The concept of **"outer space"** as a near-vacuum, devoid of air and thus dark unless lit by stars/galaxies, became more widely accepted.  

#### **20th Century: Confirming the Vacuum of Space**  
- **Source:** Universe Today. *When Did We Realize Space Was a Vacuum?* Retrieved from https://www.universetoday.com/153496/when-did-we-realize-space-was-a-vacuum/.  
- **Key Event:** By the early 1900s, experiments like **Michelson-Morley (1887)** undermined the "luminiferous aether," further solidifying that space is a vacuum. Astronauts’ observations (e.g., Yuri Gagarin in 1961) later provided direct evidence of space’s darkness.  

---

### **Conclusion**  
The shift from thinking space was "blue" to recognizing its **dark, empty nature** began **systematically in the 17th century** with telescopic and optical advancements. However, it solidified in the **19th century** as atmospheric science explained the origin of Earth’s sky color and established space as a lightless vacuum. The realization thus unfolded over **~500 years**, with milestones in observation (Galileo), physics (Newton), and experimental chemistry (Tyndall).
