## Answer

The delayed obliteration of an arterial lumen can occur through multiple mechanisms, **and** these pathways often involve contrasting or interconnected processes. **For** instance, the initial injury to the endothelial layer exposes collagen fibers, attracting platelets and fibrin **so** a traumatic thrombus forms, progressively narrowing the vessel. **But** alongside thrombosis, hemodynamic forces—such as blood flow—may tear or displace the damaged intima, creating a subintimal dissection that elevates and prolapses a flap into the lumen, physically obstructing it. **Nor** are tears to the arterial wall always necessary; an intramural hematoma might develop when blood leaks into the vessel’s media, compressing the lumen from within while leaving the outer coats intact. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive: **for** example, a flap-generated roughened surface could exacerbate thrombus growth, or a hematoma might further destabilize the intima, fostering additional pathological responses. Collectively, these interactions illustrate how varied pathological and biomechanical factors synergize to delay arterial occlusion. The thrombus, flap displacement, and hematoma each contribute distinct yet overlapping roles, with **and** the cumulative effect often outweighing any single mechanism’s isolated impact.
