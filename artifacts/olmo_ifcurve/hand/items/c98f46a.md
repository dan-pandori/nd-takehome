## Answer

The **row space** of a matrix \( A \) is defined as the set of all linear combinations of its rows, while the **nullspace** of \( A \), denoted \( \mathcal{N}(A) \), consists of all vectors \( \mathbf{x} \) such that \( A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \). These two spaces are **orthogonal** to each other because of the fundamental relationship between multiplication and dot products. When we compute \( A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \), each entry of the resulting vector is the dot product of \( \mathbf{x} \) with a corresponding row of \( A \). For \( A\mathbf{x} \) to equal the zero vector, **each row of \( A \) must be orthogonal to \( \mathbf{x} \)**. Since **the rows span the row space**, any vector in the nullspace \( \mathcal{N}(A) \) is orthogonal to **every vector in the row space**. 

To elaborate, suppose \( \mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, \dots, \mathbf{r}_m \) are the rows of \( A \). The condition \( A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \) implies:
\[
\mathbf{r}_1 \cdot \mathbf{x} = 0, \quad \mathbf{r}_2 \cdot \mathbf{x} = 0, \quad \dots, \quad \mathbf{r}_m \cdot \mathbf{x} = 0.
\]
Thus, \( \mathbf{x} \) is orthogonal to **each basis vector of the row space**. Because orthogonality is preserved under linear combinations, **\( \mathbf{x} \) is also orthogonal to any linear combination of these rows**, which spans the entire row space. This establishes the orthogonality between \( \mathcal{N}(A) \) and \( \text{Row}(A) \).

Turning to the **column space** \( \mathcal{C}(A) \)—the span of \( A \)'s columns—and the **left nullspace** \( \mathcal{N}(A^T) \), we observe a similar principle. The column space of \( A \) is precisely the *row space* of \( A^T \), since transposing \( A \) swaps rows and columns. The left nullspace \( \mathcal{N}(A^T) \) consists of vectors \( \mathbf{y} \) such that \( A^T\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0} \). Using the same logic as above but applied to \( A^T \), we see that **each column of \( A \)** (which are the rows of \( A^T \)) must be orthogonal to vectors in \( \mathcal{N}(A^T) \). 

Specifically, the equation \( A^T\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0} \) requires that the dot product of each row of \( A^T \)—**which is a column of \( A \)**—with \( \mathbf{y} \) equals zero. Thus, **every column of \( A \) is orthogonal to \( \mathbf{y} \)**, meaning \( \mathbf{y} \) is orthogonal to the entire column space \( \mathcal{C}(A) \). 

This relationship holds because:
1. **The row space of \( A^T \) is perpendicular to its nullspace \( \mathcal{N}(A^T) \)** by the initial orthogonality theorem.
2. Since \( \mathcal{C}(A) = \text{Row}(A^T) \) and \( \mathcal{N}(A^T) \) is the left nullspace of \( A \), **orthogonality directly transfers** between \( \mathcal{C}(A) \) and \( \mathcal{N}(A^T) \).

To summarize, the **nullspace** is always orthogonal to the **row space** due to the zeroing effect of \( A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \), while the **column space** and **left nullspace** inherit this orthogonality through the transpose relationship. This forms a foundational concept in linear algebra, linking the structure of matrices with geometric interpretations of their spaces. **Their interplay** reveals deeper connections between algebraic equations and spatial relationships, **enriching** our understanding of how linear transformations "act" on vectors. **They** also highlight the symmetry in duality between rows and columns when considering transposes, **which** ultimately ensures that **these orthogonalities hold universally for any matrix**.
