## Answer

The profound and irreversible transformation of Earth’s biophysical systems, as highlighted by Witze (2024), manifests distinctly in geomorphological changes documented by Nixon (2011). This systemic reconfiguration has prompted scholarly recognition through the innovative term “Anthropocene,” introduced by Stoermer and Crutzen in 2000 (Nixon, 2011). Building on this conceptual framework, Smith and Zeder (2013) advocate for a revision of geological nomenclature, proposing the formalization of the “Anthropocene epoch” to supersede the Holocene (Haraway, 2016). Such a paradigmatic shift underscores the unprecedented ecological dominance of industrial-era humanity, termed the *Anthropos*, whose activities have fundamentally altered global biogeochemical cycles, disrupted biodiversity, and reconfigured lithostratigraphic trajectories. This neologism transcends mere geological taxonomy, encapsulating interdisciplinary discourse on socio-ecological thresholds, planetary boundaries, and the ethical implications of anthropogenic-driven environmental degradation. By designating a new epoch, scholars emphasize humanity’s conversion from a passive component of Earth’s systems to an active geologic agent, whose actions irrevocably shape climatic patterns, land-use regimes, and evolutionary trajectories. The concept thereby challenges traditional frameworks of environmental governance, necessitating reevaluation of sustainable development paradigms and urgent mitigation strategies to address cascading ecological crises. This reconceptualization of the Holocene-Anthropocene transition not only redefines humanity’s role within Earth’s natural history but also compels global collaboration to navigate the complexities of a human-dominated planetary regime.
