## Answer

The delayed obliteration of the arterial lumen can arise through multiple interconnected mechanisms. First, the roughened or torn intima attracts platelets **and** fibrin, sparking a traumatic thrombus that gradually blocks the lumen. **Or**, blood flow may force the disrupted intima to tear further, **but** this displacement can create a subintimal dissection—elevating the flap to physically occlude the distal lumen **nor** allow blood to flow past. **Yet**, even when the arterial coats appear intact, an intramural hematoma might form within the media layer, compressing the lumen without obvious structural rupture. **So**, these processes—thrombosis, dissection, and hematoma—all contribute synergistically; platelet aggregation and fibrin buildup stiffen the clot, the displaced flap mechanically obstructs flow, **and** the hematoma’s pressure compromise the vessel’s internal space. **Indeed**, their combined effects, rather than occurring in isolation, **or** sometimes sequentially, lead to the gradual, layered obliteration of the arterial pathway over time.
