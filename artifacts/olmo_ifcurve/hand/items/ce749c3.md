## Answer

The delayed obliteration of an arterial lumen arises through interconnected mechanisms that reflect both structural and hemodynamic complexities. **First**, the roughened or torn intima attracts platelets, fibrin, and red blood cells, initiating traumatic thrombosis. **This** cascade progressively narrows the lumen through clot formation. **However**, a distinct pathway involves dynamic blood flow interacting with the injured intima: **if** a tear occurs, the sheer force of flowing blood elevates the distal flap, **and** this movement creates a subintimal dissection. **Such** subdissection displaces the intimal layer into the lumen, physically obstructing blood flow. **Moreover**, neither the intima nor the adventitia need show obvious disruption for luminal closure to occur. An intramural hematoma—localized bleeding within the arterial wall’s medial layer—may develop independently, **even when** external structural integrity appears intact. **This** hematoma exerts compressive forces on adjacent tissue, slowly narrowing the lumen from within. **Thus**, these mechanisms—the thrombotic, the dynamically displaced flap, **nor** the insidious intramural bleeding—demonstrate how delayed occlusion can emerge even when immediate structural catastrophes, such as complete intimal tears, are absent. **Ultimately**, the interplay of these processes underscores the multifactorial nature of arterial lumen obliteration over time.
