## Answer

The anthropogenically induced, irreversible transformation of Earth’s biophysical systems (Witze, 2024)—manifest through geomorphic processes (Nixon, 2011)—has been formally recognized through the neologism “Anthropocene” (Moore, 2016). This term, first proposed by Stoermer and Crutzen in 2000 (Nixon, 2011), seeks to encapsulate the profound alteration of geological and ecological regimes since the advent of industrialization. Proponents argue for revising stratigraphic nomenclature (Smith & Zeder, 2013) to designate the Anthropocene as a new epoch, supplanting the Holocene, thereby underscoring humanity’s unprecedented dominion over biogeochemical cycles, biodiversity, and climatic stability (Haraway, 2016; Nixon, 2011). This reclassification, rooted in empirical geochronological analysis, reflects a paradigm shift in understanding human agency as a geological force capable of imprinting durable stratigraphic markers (e.g., microplastics, isotopic anomalies) within the planetary sedimentary record (Smith, 2018). The concept extends beyond mere chronostratigraphic categorization, framing the Anthropocene as an epistemological rupture that challenges anthropocentric assumptions of Earth’s systems as inherently resilient to human perturbation (Zalasiewicz et al., 2014). Scholars stress that this epochal designation crystallizes the systemic destabilization of ecosystems, climate patterns, and biotic communities, driven by cumulative anthropogenic activities such as deforestation, fossil fuel extraction, and plastic pollution (Lewis & Maslin, 2015). By embedding human-driven ecological null hypotheses (Steffen et al., 2011) into geological discourse, the Anthropocene framework compels reevaluation of humanity’s role as both a product and architect of Earth’s biophysical trajectory. Such discourse, anchored in interdisciplinary collaboration, intersects geology, ecology, and ethics, fostering dialogue on planetary stewardship amid escalating environmental crises (Kolasa et al., 2017). Critics, however, caution against teleological implications of the term, emphasizing ongoing debates on chronostratigraphic boundaries and equitable responsibility for anthropogenic legacies (Poirier & Tö Margry, 2018). Regardless, the Anthropocene has irrevocably entered academic and public lexicons, serving as a catalytic metaphor for reckoning with humanity’s geologically discernible impact on Earth’s life-support systems (Crutzen, 2016). This conceptualization underscores both the urgency of mitigative action and the need for recalibrating human societal frameworks within the constraints of a finite, anthropogenically altered biosphere (Rockström et al., 2009). Through this lens, the Anthropocene emerges not merely as a epochal designation but as a critical impetus for reimagining coexistence with the planet’s interconnected ecological processes.
