### **When Did Humans Realize Space Is a Dark Void?**  
The shift from the idea of a "blue" celestial realm to understanding space as a dark void with stars and galaxies unfolded over centuries, driven by scientific inquiry:  

#### **Ancient to Medieval Eras**  
- **Aristotelian Cosmology (4th century BCE):** Aristotle proposed a geocentric universe with concentric spheres (e.g., the "ether" beyond Earth’s air) that might have been imagined as luminous or blue, akin to Earth’s sky.  

#### **Early Modern Discoveries (16th–17th Centuries)**  
- **Galileo Galilei (1609):** His telescopic observations (e.g., Jupiter’s moons, lunar craters) challenged the notion of a "perfect" celestial sphere, suggesting space could be **physically similar to Earth**.  
- **Isaac Newton (1687):** His laws of motion and gravity implied a **vacuum** in space (no "ether" medium), as celestial bodies moved without resistance.  

#### **The Aether Debate (19th Century)**  
- **Michelson-Morley Experiment (1887):** Failed to detect "aether winds," undermining the idea of a luminous or conductive ether. This **pushed science toward accepting space as a true vacuum**.  

#### **Einstein and the Birth of Modern Cosmology (Early 20th Century)**  
- **Albert Einstein (1905):** Special relativity abolished the need for an aether, solidifying space as a **vacuum**—implying no blue scattering of light and an inherently **dark backdrop**.  
- **Edwin Hubble (1920s–1930s):** Observed galaxies far beyond the Milky Way, revealing the universe’s vast, **empty expanses** between stars and galaxies (now called "voids").  

#### **Public Perception vs. Scientific Consensus**  
- **Early Misconceptions:** Many believed space might resemble Earth’s blue sky due to confusion with atmospheric phenomena.  
- **Space Age (1960s):** Astronaut photographs of the Moon and astronaut accounts (e.g., seeing a **Black Earth** from space) cemented the reality of space’s darkness to the public.  

### **Conclusion**  
By the **early 20th century**, scientists had coalesced on the idea of a dark, empty universe punctuated by stars and galaxies. However, widespread acceptance among the general public came later, with the **Space Age** providing irrefutable visual evidence. The transition from "blue sky" myths to modern understanding hinged on challenging ancient concepts of the ether and embracing vacuum physics.
