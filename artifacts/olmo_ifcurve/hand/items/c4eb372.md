### When did humans realize space is a dark void?  
For much of history, many cultures associated the "heavens" with divine, luminous realms (e.g., the "celestial sphere" in ancient astronomy). However, the shift toward understanding space as a dark, empty void began during the **Scientific Revolution** (16th–17th centuries):  

1. **Early theories of vacuum**:  
   - **Evangelista Torricelli** (1643) demonstrated air’s weight using a mercury barometer, suggesting space could be a vacuum—a concept later supported by **Isaac Newton** (1687), who argued that celestial bodies move in the "aether," a hypothetical medium. This implied no atmosphere to scatter light, hinting at darkness.  

2. **Optics and atmospheric science**:  
   - **Isaac Newton** (1670s) showed that sunlight is composed of all visible wavelengths, and shorter (blue) wavelengths scatter more in the atmosphere, explaining Earth’s blue sky. Extrapolating this, scientists realized that **beyond Earth’s atmosphere**, there would be no scattering—resulting in darkness interrupted only by distant stars and galaxies.  

3. **Empirical observations**:  
   - Telescopic observations, like **Galileo Galilei’s** (early 1600s), revealed the Moon’s cratered surface and Jupiter’s moons, challenging the idea of a "transparent, blue celestial sphere." By the **19th century**, experiments by **John Tyndall** (1850s) confirmed air molecules scatter light, solidifying the understanding that space’s darkness stems from its near-vacuum.  

4. **Space exploration (1960s)**:  
   - Astronauts’ firsthand accounts of the "blackness of space" (e.g., **Edwin "Buzz" Aldrin** during Apollo 11, 1969) provided definitive proof, aligning with earlier scientific theories.  

**Key takeaway**: While speculative ideas existed earlier, the **mid-17th to 19th centuries** saw systematic evidence disproving the notion of a blue "celestial sky." The synthesis of vacuum theory, optics, and experiment cemented the modern understanding by the 1800s.
