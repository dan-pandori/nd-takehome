## Answer

The row space of a matrix **A** is orthogonal to its nullspace because any vector **x** in the nullspace satisfies **Ax = 0**, meaning the dot product of **x** with each row of **A** is zero. Since the row space is formed by all linear combinations of **A**’s rows, **x** must also be orthogonal to every vector in the row space. Thus, vectors in the nullspace cannot share any non-zero components with the row space, making **they** orthogonal subspaces.  

For the column space (spanned by **A**’s columns) and the left nullspace of **A** (vectors **y** with **A^T y = 0**), similar logic applies. Here, **y** must be orthogonal to every column of **A**, as seen in **A^T y = 0**. Because the column space includes all combinations of **A**’s columns, **y** remains orthogonal to this space. Hence, they are orthogonal, just **as** the row space and nullspace are.  

This duality arises because transposition swaps the roles of rows and columns: **the column space of A corresponds to the row space of A^T**, and the left nullspace of **A** is **A^T**’s nullspace. **They** inherit the same orthogonality principle: **their** components cannot overlap except at zero. In **total**, **three key relationships** involve these orthogonal pairs, ensuring that row-nullspace and column-left-nullspace duality defines the geometry of linear systems. **Their** interactions reflect how matrix dimensions and subspaces interrelate through transposition and column-row symmetry.
