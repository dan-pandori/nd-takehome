To find the amplitude and initial phase (\(\varphi\) or \(\phi\)) for a spring pendulum performing undamped harmonic oscillations, we first identify that the motion of such a system is described by simple harmonic motion due to the lack of damping and the restoring nature of the spring force.

Given:
- Mass \(m\)
- Spring stiffness \(k\)
- Initial coordinate \(x_0\)
- Initial velocity \(V_0\)

### Amplitude

The amplitude \(A\) of oscillation for a system undergoing undamped harmonic motion, where we only have initial position and velocity, can be derived by understanding the energy conservation of the system. However, in the case of a linear spring-mass system without external forces (like friction or air resistance), the amplitude of motion can be determined from the energy stored in the system initially divided by the total energy in the undamped harmonic oscillator.

Nevertheless, without additional information or a specific energy balance question, let's focus on the velocity component to directly find the amplitude. The amplitude of a displacement as a function of time for an undamped harmonic oscillator is directly related to its velocity through the following relationship:

\[v(t) = \pm A \omega \cos(\omega t + \varphi)\]

where \(v(t)\) is the velocity, \(A\) is the amplitude, \(\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\) is the angular frequency, and \(\varphi\) is the initial phase. Based on the initial conditions provided:
- At \(t = 0\), the velocity \(V = V_0\).
- There's no direct information on the position at \(t = 0\) affecting the amplitude calculation directly, but let's note that \(x_0\) influences the initial phase \(\varphi\).

Considering that there's no damping, we can set the energy condition (potential energy at \(x_0\) is equal to half of the maximum kinetic energy) where:

\[\frac{1}{2} k x_0^2 = \frac{1}{2} m A^2\]

From the given kinetic and potential initial conditions:
\[A = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} x_0\]

This suggests the initial displacement doesn't directly contribute to amplitude in this energy definition but does help to define the system's initial state where \(x_0\) directly affects the initial phase \(\varphi\) or \(\phi\) rather than the amplitude as formulated in simple undamped harmonic motion problems (unless considering the system's behavior beyond its immediate definition of \(\omega\)).

For the initial phase \(\varphi\), since we have the initial velocity \(V_0\), we relate it to the amplitude \(\omega\) and angles in the cosine function as given in the geometrical description of the harmonics (related to the equilibrium position, with max velocities at the equilibrium point pointing towards \(\varphi = 0\) at \(t = 0\) and vice versa at half cycle):

\[\phi = \begin{cases} 
\arccos\left(\frac{V_0}{A \omega}\right) & \text{if } V_0 > 0 \\
2\pi - \arccos\left(\frac{V_0}{A \omega}\right) & \text{if } V_0 < 0
\end{cases}\]

Considering \(V_0\) and knowing \(A\), we can define \(\varphi\) accurately, but the direct formula includes understanding \(V_0\)'s sign and relation to \(A \omega\) based on given \(x_0\) and derived \(A\).

- The amplitude \(A\) of oscillation is \(A = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} x_0\).
- The initial phase \(\varphi\) is determined through \(\varphi = \arccos\left(\frac{V_0}{A \omega}\right)\) (or \(2\pi - \arccos\left(\frac{V_0}{A \omega}\right)\) if \(V_0 < 0\)) after determining \(A\).

This approach assumes energy conservation or direct initial condition matching to harmonic oscillation forms, clarifying the problem's nuances emphasize the need for context in harmonic motion analysis.
