\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the knockout shows, and what it does not.} The pre-registered verdict is that the classical families
\emph{need exposure}: from a \sig-free base, this EI did not acquire them in 16 rounds, while every full-exposure seed
did, and a 1\% dose was enough. The verdict is about one algorithm and one horizon. It does not say that RL cannot
produce \sig{}. The knockout models wrote \sig{} themselves, and one ladder spread it from round 9 onwards. A null
result like this one has four readings, and the diagnostics bear on each.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{\sig{} is out of the policy's reach.} This is false for four of six bases, which write \sig{} proofs of
family members at large $k$. It cannot be excluded for the two certified bases (s0, s3) at RL's budget, though even
there it is not excluded at larger budgets.
\item \emph{EI explores too little.} EI spends 32 attempts per training theorem per round. On the 123 strict training
targets that is 3,936 attempts per round. At the knockout bases' pooled rates (0 to $5.4\times10^{-6}$ per attempt on
held-out members) it yields at most 0.02 expected \sig{} proofs per round, or at most 0.34 over 16 rounds. Most knockout
ladders therefore had little or nothing to train on. The three ladders that solved any strict training target by
round 16 (s1, s2, s5: 5, 1 and 2 targets) are the three whose ladders wrote the free reductio earliest (rounds 9, 12
and 2).
\item \emph{EI amplifies rare finds too slowly.} Knockout seed 1 multiplied its free reductio more than tenfold in
eight extra rounds, yet its held-out set stayed at 5 members. Amplification there was real but narrow: the model became reliable
on the members it could already reach, without generalising to the family. A single seeded proof did nothing in one
round. The test of seeding at volume is EI with the 1,550 dose records in replay instead of pretraining, and it is
not run.
\item \emph{The knockout made the bases worse learners in general.} This is not supported. RL's compute-matched gain
on the 322 evaluation theorems is the same in both arms ($+21$ to $+37$), and the knockout's general-ability cost sits
in theorems that need \sig.
\end{enumerate}
Readings 2 and 3 together describe an \emph{amplification threshold}. EI turns a composition the base produces at
moderate rates into a capability within a few rounds, and it does nothing visible with one the base produces at
$10^{-6}$. The dose arm locates the threshold between 0\% and 1\% of pretraining records for this organism and budget,
and the 0.1\% arm would narrow it.

\paragraph{Relation to controlled studies.} \citet{tsilivis2025rlntp} show, on parity, that RL after next-token
pretraining amplifies a rare long-reasoning demonstration efficiently as long as its share of the data is not
exponentially small. Our 1\% dose sits on the efficient side. Our knockout leaves the composition at about $10^{-6}$ per
attempt, which for EI at $k=32$ behaves like ``exponentially small''. Zero exposure was not tested there, as far as the
quotes we verified go. \citet{zhang2025interplay} find, in a 100M model trained from scratch, that 0\% or 0.1\% exposure
to a context in pretraining gives no transfer after RL and 1\% enhances it. That is close to our dose pattern, for a
context rather than an inference rule. \citet{abdulsalam2026rewrite} remove shortcuts from pretraining in a rewrite
grammar and report RL solving buckets on which the pretrained policy scores 0\% at pass@1024. In our organism, a
pass@1024 of zero is compatible with per-attempt rates of $10^{-6}$, which a million attempts reveal. Their new
behaviour is a new action type, while ours is a composition of two rules the base already uses. More importantly, the
algorithm that created it there was GRPO. Rejection-sampling fine-tuning, the family our EI belongs to, plateaued, and
they attribute the difference to selectivity rather than exploration volume. Our null may therefore be specific to EI.
The two results are not in conflict, but neither settles the other. A GRPO arm from the knockout bases is the obvious
test.

\paragraph{An evidence ladder for ``RL created it''.} The measurement half of the paper suggests a reporting standard,
in increasing order of strength:
\begin{enumerate}
\item equal-$k$ success;
\item a compute-matched base budget, reported as a curve over defensible allocations;
\item RL-free controls with matched training;
\item a Clopper--Pearson certificate against the base at that budget;
\item an intervention on what the base saw.
\end{enumerate}
In our full-exposure seeds the claim shrinks at each step: 54/51/60 theorems at equal $k$, 21/20/14 under every
allocation, 20/14/8 net of replay at $\Kpub$, and 4 (theorem, seed) pairs on 3 theorems surviving every control, of
which one theorem is classical-only. The intervention then shows that, for one composition, exposure in pretraining
decides whether this RL acquires it. Each step is cheap in a small organism and expensive in a large one. Steps 2 and 3
are affordable at any scale and are rarely reported.

\paragraph{Implications for evaluation and data filtering.} Two practical points follow, with the caveat that they come
from a small organism. First, a capability that is absent at $k=256$ or $k=1{,}024$ can still be present at $10^{-6}$
per attempt, and RL is the kind of process that can amplify such rates. Evaluations that aim to bound a capability need
rare-event estimates, such as large-$k$ sampling, likelihoods of known solutions or importance sampling, not pass@$k$
at modest $k$. Second, the knockout is a data-filtering intervention. Removing a composition from pretraining
prevented its acquisition by this RL within this budget, but 1\% leakage undid the filter. A filter that leaves even a
small fraction of the composition in place may not slow later RL at all.

\paragraph{Interface.} An earlier, smaller organism of the same project, a 3.2M-parameter model trained on proofs of
at most six lines, had 29 theorems that expert iteration solved and that one base seed solved 0 times in 400,000
whole-proof attempts. A base of the same size acting through the proof-state interface reaches 28 of them (at least one
accepted proof in up to 400,000 attempts; at EI's own bar of $\hat p \geq 0.01$ it clears 21 and 14 on its two
seeds). There were only two seeds per arm, and the state cannot be separated from step-by-step generation. Whether RL ``created'' something can
depend on the interface through which the base is asked. We leave that axis to future work, at the 9.56M scale.
