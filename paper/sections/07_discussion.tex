\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the knockout shows.} Without \sig{} in pretraining or replay, 16 rounds of this EI acquired no classical
family on any of six seeds. With it, full-exposure seeds gained 8--69 strict held-out theorems during RL, and a 1\%
dose was enough. The pre-registered verdict, \textsc{needs exposure}, holds for converse contraposition, the family the
full bases mostly had before RL; for the others it is undetermined at six seeds. Beyond the waiting-time null, the
knockout ladders did raise their own rate of the free positive reductio: three reached a strict training theorem
sooner than their base rates predict. They did not raise it to a family.

\paragraph{Four readings of the null.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{\sig{} is out of the policy's reach.} This is false for five of six bases, which wrote \sig{} proofs of
strict theorems at large $k$. For seed 0, certified on a pooled mean rate at one budget with an 8\% margin, it cannot be
excluded at that budget.
\item \emph{EI samples too little.} At 32 attempts per theorem, EI's per-round budget on the strict theorems is about
4,000 attempts, against base rates near $10^{-6}$. That explains why most ladders saw nothing. It does not explain
everything: three ladders reached strict theorems sooner than this null predicts ($p=0.006$).
\item \emph{EI amplifies rare finds too slowly, or too narrowly.} Seed 5 wrote a free positive reductio in round 2 and
did not spread it in 14 more rounds. Seed 1 multiplied its free reductio more than tenfold in eight extra rounds and
still proved the same five held-out theorems. One mechanism: in EI a single new proof enters a fine-tuning mix of about
70,000 RL records (up to four proofs for each of 4,495 theorems, each repeated four times) plus 20,000 replay records.
Under a group-normalised policy gradient, a single success among 32 attempts would get an advantage of
$\sqrt{31} \approx 5.6$ standard deviations.
\item \emph{The knockout made the bases worse learners in general.} The general costs are a few theorems out of
hundreds, and the knockout ladders' gains elsewhere ($\dcov$ means 29.8 vs 35.0, $p = 0.13$) are not clearly smaller.
The dose arm restores acquisition while changing the data much less. This reading is not excluded, but it is not
needed.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Earlier organisms point the same way, and further.} The project studied related patterns in smaller
organisms. These are 3.2M--85M-parameter models that write whole proofs of at most six lines, trained on reductio-free
or depth-limited sets. They were judged by an older in-house checker, with Lean confirmation where stated, and their
reviewed results carry that label.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The base rate decided ignition.} Four of eleven reductio-free bases emitted the strict reductio before
any RL ($8\times10^{-6}$--$2.3\times10^{-4}$ per sample), and exactly those four ignited under EI. The other seven
stayed at 0 of 606 targets. First proofs arrived about when $1/(rkN)$ predicts: rounds 1, 2, 6 and 6 against 0.2, 1.7,
2.6 and 6.2.
\item \textbf{Exploration knobs did little; injected proofs did a lot.} One round at $k=128$ or $T=1$ changed almost
nothing. One fine-tuning step on a sibling's found proofs ignited all 11 dead arms (reductio and depth-3). A single injected proof ignited the
reductio on two dead seeds within one to two rounds, reaching 44 and 65 targets by round 8.
\item \textbf{Size did not change it.} At 3.2M, 25M and 85M parameters, rates did not rise with size. A rare 85M draw
that read 0 of 300 after eight rounds produced its first proof in round 13 when continued.
\item \textbf{GRPO ignited where EI did not.} On two draws with no depth-3 proof in 600,000 base samples, GRPO
acquired a depth-3 box-nesting habit in 12 of 12 arms, where paired EI did so in 1 of 3 (Lean-confirmed; the
checkpoints are lost).
\end{itemize}
In the present organism, a base pretrained with no \texttt{DN} at all learned excluded middle from 16 demonstrations
(35--37 of 39 members on three seeds). On the full bases, replay alone, or 16 proofs of other theorems, gave none. Taken together, in this project a pattern the base
produces rarely or not at all is acquired when it is injected, even as one proof given a few rounds, or found by GRPO.
EI from its own samples acquires it when the base already produces it at a rate that EI's sampling can hit. The knockout
fits this account: 1\% exposure put the bases above that rate, and none of six knockout bases reached it in 16 rounds.
``Needs exposure'' should be read as ``needs exposure, or demonstrations, or a different search, for this EI''.

\paragraph{EI or RL?} The evidence on whether the null is specific to EI cuts both ways. Against: Interplay's 0\%
exposure null used GRPO \citep{zhang2025interplay}, theory predicts a barrier for any outcome-reward policy gradient
beyond the base's support \citep{mousavihosseini2026barrier,xie2024xpo}, and GRPO on a Lean prover rarely uplifts rare
correct proofs \citep{he2025unlikely}. For: RFT plateaued where GRPO built new shortcuts \citep{abdulsalam2026rewrite},
iterated RFT failed where RL composed functions \citep{yuan2025fgx}, and GRPO ignited from zero where EI mostly did not
in our earlier organism. GRPO from the knockout bases, at matched samples, is the decisive missing experiment.

\paragraph{An amplification threshold?} A natural hypothesis is that EI amplifies a composition once its per-theorem
rate on some training theorems is high enough for 32 attempts per round to hit it within a few rounds, and acquires a
family once that holds on enough members. Knockout seed 1 fits: it amplified the members its base proved at about
$10^{-4}$, not the family. But two doses cannot tell a threshold from a graded response. The rate in question is not the
median likelihood of known proofs: dose seeds 0 and 2 ($-25.3$ and $-24.5$ nats) overlap knockout seed 1 ($-26.0$), and
their outcomes differ by 28--56 theorems. The 0.1\% dose and per-theorem rates on the training theorems would test it.

\paragraph{What transfers to larger models.} The protocol transfers: budgets matched to RL's compute and reported as
curves, RL-free controls, certificates. So does the waiting-time logic, in which a first success is expected after
about $1/(rkN)$ rounds, with $N$ the number of training problems that need the step. With $10^4$--$10^5$ prompts and
$k$ of 8--64, LLM RL can find far rarer compositions than our 123 strict theorems allow, if a comparable share of
prompts needs them. The numbers do not transfer: the 1\% threshold, the $10^{-6}$ rates, the EI dynamics. Two practical
points follow, with that caveat.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Evaluation.} Absence at modest $k$ is not absence. Our knockout bases fail at $k=256$, yet succeed at
pooled rates near $10^{-6}$, and on a few theorems near $10^{-4}$. EI amplified the latter, on one seed, and not the
former.
\item \textbf{Data filtering.} Removing a composition from pretraining kept this EI from acquiring it for 16 rounds,
but 1\% of records restored it. We did not test the leakage levels a real filter leaves. The stronger threat,
fine-tuning on demonstrations, defeated a similar filter in our earlier organism with a single proof.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{An evidence hierarchy for ``RL created it''.} In increasing strength:
\begin{enumerate}
\item success at equal $k$;
\item a compute-matched base budget, reported as a curve over defensible allocations;
\item RL-free controls with matched training;
\item a certificate against the base at that budget;
\item an intervention on what the base saw.
\end{enumerate}
For our full-exposure seeds the claim shrinks at each step (Table~\ref{tab:budgets}). The intervention then shows that,
for one composition, exposure decides whether this EI acquires it. Steps 2 and 3 cost about as much as the RL run they
audit, so they are affordable wherever that run was; they are rarely reported.
