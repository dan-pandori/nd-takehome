\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reinforcement learning against a verifier (RLVR) has become the standard way to improve reasoning models. Whether it
teaches them anything new is contested. \citet{yue2025doesrl} find that base models overtake their RL-trained
descendants in pass@$k$ as $k$ grows, which suggests that RL mostly sharpens a distribution that already contained the
answers. \citet{karan2025power} reach most of RL's gain on a mathematics benchmark by sampling the base model from a
sharpened distribution, with no training. Other work reports RL solving problems the base never solved
\citep{abdulsalam2026rewrite,liu2025prorl}. The disagreement is partly about words. ``The base never solved it'' is a
statement about a sample size. A base whose per-attempt success probability $p$ on a theorem is $10^{-6}$ will
almost never solve it in 1,024 attempts, yet the capability is there in a sense that matters for safety and for
evaluation.

We argue that a claim that RL \emph{created} a capability needs three things that are rarely all present:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A budget.} Every proof has positive probability under a softmax policy. For our models at random
  initialisation the known proofs alone bound the expected attempts needed from above at $e^{289}$ to $e^{3{,}228}$
  (\S\ref{sec:measure}). So ``the base cannot'' must mean ``the base cannot within $K$ attempts'', and $K$ should be
  tied to what RL spent.
  \item \textbf{Controls.} RL's training steps also replay pretraining data and continue optimisation. An RL-free
  control with the same training must fail where RL succeeds.
  \item \textbf{An intervention on the base.} The strongest evidence that RL created something is that it appears
  from a base that provably never saw its ingredients. Studies of large models can rarely edit pretraining.
\end{enumerate}

We build a model organism where all three are possible. It is a 9.56M-parameter transformer prover for propositional
logic, pretrained from scratch on 155,000 synthetic natural-deduction proofs. A proof-state environment shows it the
goal and hypotheses and asks for one step at a time. Lean~4 alone decides whether a proof counts. Because the model is
small and the data synthetic, we can (i) give the base millions of attempts per theorem, (ii) compute the teacher-forced
probability of any known proof, and (iii) remove one inference composition from pretraining and retrain.

The composition we remove is the classical reductio \sig{}: double-negation elimination (\texttt{DN}, Lean's
\texttt{Classical.byContradiction}) applied to a line built by negation introduction. To prove a positive goal $G$
classically, one assumes $\neg G$, derives a contradiction, concludes $\neg\neg G$, and eliminates the double
negation. Both rules stay in the knockout data, used separately. Only their composition goes. Six classical theorem
families (excluded middle, two Peirce families, and the classical members of the converse-contraposition,
negated-conditional and De Morgan families) need a \texttt{DN} step on a double negation the proof itself builds.
Their held-out members test whether expert iteration (EI) acquires the composition from a base that did not see it.

\paragraph{Main result.} Within 16 rounds of EI, no \sig-free (``knockout'') base acquired any classical family:
0 of 6 seeds reached the pre-registered bar of half a family's held-out members, against 6 of 6 full-exposure seeds
(one-sided Boschloo $p = 0.00024$, Fig.~\ref{fig:endpoint}). Counting only families that were not already at the bar
before RL, it was 5/6 vs 0/6 ($p = 0.0032$). Putting \sig{} back into 1\% of the pretraining records restored
acquisition on 3 of 3 seeds. The knockout bases were not \sig-free in behaviour. Four of six wrote \sig{} proofs of
held-out family members at large sample sizes, and only two are certified out of reach at RL's compute budget. The one
knockout ladder that made progress, followed to 24 rounds, solved mostly members its own base could already reach
within RL's budget (three of its four converse-contraposition members). In this
organism EI amplified a composition the base already produced, however rarely. It did not build the composition, in
16 rounds, from a base that had never seen it.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An organism for causal create-versus-elicit questions} (\S\ref{sec:setup}): a from-scratch prover with
  a sound external judge, exact likelihoods, and pretraining data that can be edited one inference composition at a
  time. Everything is open: code, data, checkpoints and per-arm compute.
  \item \textbf{A measurement protocol, applied end to end} (\S\ref{sec:measure}). It has budgets in base attempts
  matched to RL's compute, under five allocations, so the ``created'' set becomes a curve in $K$. It adds $k$-to-solve
  intervals from sampling and a known-proof likelihood estimator, calibrated down to $p \approx 10^{-4}$,
  Clopper--Pearson certificates of unreachability, and RL-free controls. Of 51--60 theorems per seed that RL
  ``creates'' at equal $k$, 14--21 survive every budget allocation, and 4 (theorem, seed) pairs on 3 theorems survive
  every control.
  \item \textbf{A pre-registered composition knockout} (\S\ref{sec:results}) with a dose arm and diagnostics: base
  rates at $10^6$ attempts, certificates, the likelihood of known \sig{} proofs along every ladder, and an extended
  ladder. They separate ``absent'' from ``rare''.
  \item \textbf{Null and negative results at their strength.} A power-sampling null that fails its own positive
  control, so we report it as specific to one sampler; a seeded-round null; and the knockout's costs to general
  ability.
  \item \textbf{Test-time compute before and after RL} (\S\ref{sec:ttc}): guided step redraws and tree search close
  roughly half of the gap between the base and the RL model where we measured it, and add little after RL.
\end{enumerate}

Everything here is about one organism: 9.56M parameters, propositional logic, one RL algorithm (expert iteration) and
16 rounds. Section~\ref{sec:limits} lists what that does not license.
